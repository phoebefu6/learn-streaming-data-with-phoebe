Part 1 · covers the problem

The batch day, hour by hour 6 min live

Nothing in the nightly pipeline is broken. Every number it publishes is exactly right. The problem is purely WHEN it publishes them.

Live Freshness and correctness are two different axes 4 min ▶

The nightly dashboard scores perfectly on one axis and terribly on the other. Everything it shows is correct: totals reconciled, duplicates cleaned, late records folded in. But its freshness is whatever the clock says minus the last batch run - at 2pm, that is 14 hours.

Streaming systems make the opposite trade by default. They can show you an order seconds after it happens, and in doing so they inherit every mess the batch job used to clean up overnight: retries that arrive twice, orders that arrive late, records that land in the wrong hour. Fresh but sloppy is the starting point, not the goal.

  • Batch: correct, then eventually fresh (tomorrow).
  • Naive streaming: fresh, then eventually correct (if you do the work).
  • The craft of this course: fresh AND correct, on purpose.
Real world

A retail team ships a "real-time" sales feed in a week and celebrates. A month later finance reconciles it against the ledger and finds it over-reports by 3 to 4 percent - producer retries were counted twice. The feed was fresh the whole time. Nobody had asked whether it was right.

Self-study Why the batch job exists at all 4 min ▶

The nightly batch is not a legacy embarrassment. It is a genuinely good design for its constraints: it sees the WHOLE day before it aggregates, so late arrivals, retries and corrections have all landed by the time it runs. Completeness is cheap when you are willing to wait.

That is the standing tension. A batch job answers with everything known at run time. A stream answers NOW, with only what has arrived so far. Every technique in sessions 2 through 5 - idempotency, event-time windows, watermarks, replay - is a way of buying back, at 2pm, the completeness the batch job gets for free at 2am.

Part 2 · covers events

An event is a fact, not a row 6 min live

Before any tooling, one mental shift. The Kafka documentation defines it plainly: an event records the fact that something happened. Facts do not change. That single property is what everything else is built on.

Live Why immutability is the load-bearing property 4 min ▶

Because a fact never changes, you can hand the same event to five different consumers and they can each build their own view of the world - a revenue total, a fraud score, an inventory count - without coordinating with each other. And because the facts are kept in order, any of those views can be rebuilt from scratch by replaying them.

Martin Kleppmann's framing is the cleanest: the database you read from is just a cached view of the event log. When something goes wrong, you replay the events in the same order and reconstruct exactly what happened. That idea returns in session 5 as your correction mechanism, and in session 6 as an entire architecture.

Real world

Bank statements have worked this way for centuries. Nobody edits last Tuesday's transaction when a refund happens - a new correcting entry is appended. Accountants discovered append-only logs long before software did.

Self-study Event time versus arrival time - the seed of sessions 4 and 5 3 min ▶

Every event in tonight's simulator carries two timestamps. WHEN it happened (the customer tapped pay at 23:41) and WHEN the system heard about it (maybe 23:41, maybe 2:50am if the store's tablet was offline). For on-time events the two are the same and nobody cares. For late ones they diverge, and which timestamp you aggregate by decides whether your hourly chart tells the truth.

Hold the distinction; do not solve it yet. It becomes the whole of session 4 (windows) and session 5 (watermarks).

Part 3 · covers the decision

Most dashboards do not need this 6 min live

An honest streaming course starts by shrinking its own market. The evidence says most analytical workloads are small, recent-biased, and served perfectly well by batch.

Live The numbers that deflate the hype 4 min ▶

Jordan Tigani, a founding engineer of BigQuery, published the uncomfortable measurements: among customers actually paying for the service, 90 percent of queries processed less than 100MB of data, and heavy users' median total storage was under 100GB. He also measured the recency bias - data more than a week old is roughly 20 times less likely to be queried. Most "big data real-time" needs are neither big nor real-time.

And streaming's price is not only infrastructure. Gunnar Morling, who builds these systems for a living, puts the cost where it belongs: the price is complexity - reasoning about streaming joins, late data, and reprocessing is genuinely harder than reasoning about a nightly job.

  • The steelman is real too. Fraud detection, anomaly detection, and rule-based alerting are the canonical cases where seconds change the outcome - a fraud block after the money leaves is worthless.
  • And once teams taste it, they keep it. Morling again: experience a data freshness of a second or two in your warehouse and you never want to go back.

The test to carry: name the decision, name who makes it, and name what changes if they make it 12 hours sooner. If nothing changes, a nightly batch was the correct engineering answer, not a compromise.

Build-along

Read the 2pm dashboard honestly ★ 22 min · everyone builds

This is the simulator you will upgrade for the rest of the course. One deterministic day of coffee-shop orders. Tonight it runs the way most companies actually run: a nightly batch, viewed at 2pm.

Read the two headline numbers together, never alone. Freshness lag says 14h - the batch covered through midnight and it is now 2pm. Accuracy says "showing yesterday" because there is nothing of today on screen at all: $0 shown of the $3,723 that has really happened.

Look at the chart. The dashed outlines are what really happened hour by hour (by event time). The solid bars are what the dashboard displays. Tonight the solid bars are simply absent - that emptiness IS the 14 hours.

Now argue the batch job's side out loud: everything it DID show this morning was exactly right, retries deduplicated and stragglers included, because it waited for the whole day to land before counting. Old but honest. Hold on to that standard - next session, the moment you flip the stream on, you will watch freshness arrive and honesty leave.

Why the numbers are trustworthy: the order generator is a fixed-seed teaching simulation, but every figure on that panel is genuinely computed from the generated events - nothing is scripted. When a number moves in a later session, it moves because the semantics you switched on changed the arithmetic.

Homework

Before session 2 15 min

  • Pick ONE dashboard you actually use. Write down: when is it built, how old is it when you look at it, and what is the freshest decision anyone makes from it.
  • Run the three-zone test on it: does acting within seconds change any outcome? Write the one-sentence verdict: stream, batch, or the middle zone.
  • List three things about your dashboard's numbers that the overnight batch quietly fixes (think: retries, late records, corrections). That list is exactly what you will be fixing by hand in sessions 3 to 5.
Check yourself

Three questions

1. At 2pm, what is actually wrong with the nightly batch dashboard?

The batch job waited for the whole day, so it cleaned retries and caught stragglers. Its failure is freshness, not correctness - that is the axis streaming attacks.

2. What is an event, in the sense this course uses the word?

The Kafka docs' own definition: an event records the fact that something happened. Facts never change, which is why they can be replayed and shared safely.

3. Which of these most clearly justifies streaming?

Fraud blocking is the canonical case where acting within seconds changes the outcome. The other two are correctness-mandatory, freshness-optional - batch territory.

Answer all three to see your score.
